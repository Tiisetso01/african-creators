# Medical Billing — Frictions and Pain Points

## Friction 1 — Claim Rejections (Biggest Revenue Leak)

Every rejection is money that was earned but not collected — until someone follows it up.

**Why claims get rejected:**

| Rejection Reason | How Common | What Has to Happen |
|-----------------|-----------|-------------------|
| Wrong ICD-10 code | Very common | Billing person investigates, corrects code, resubmits |
| Code combination invalid | Common | Certain diagnosis + procedure combos are not allowed — must recode |
| Pre-auth missing | Common | Was never obtained OR not captured on the claim — must appeal |
| Pre-auth expired | Common | Auth obtained but treatment was outside the validity period |
| Patient not on plan | Occasional | Patient's plan changed, cancelled, or lapsed — must bill patient directly |
| Benefits exhausted | Occasional | Patient has used up annual limit — must bill patient for full amount |
| Duplicate claim | Occasional | Same claim submitted twice — must prove it is not a duplicate |
| Provider not registered | Rare but costly | Doctor not registered on that patient's specific plan — must re-register |

**The real problem:**
Each rejection requires investigation, correction, and resubmission. A practice with 50 patients a day generates hundreds of claims a week. Even a 10% rejection rate is dozens of rejected claims that need manual attention.

Most small practices do not follow up every rejection. They follow up the big ones and write off the rest. That is lost revenue that never gets recovered.

## Friction 2 — Pre-Authorisation Calls

**Time cost:** 15–45 minutes per call, on hold with the medical aid.

A practice doing 5–10 pre-auths a day spends 1–4 hours on hold. That is a billing person who cannot do anything else during that time.

**Additional problems:**
- Auth obtained verbally but not documented properly — rejected when claim is submitted
- Auth obtained for wrong procedure code — claim rejected
- Auth expires before treatment happens — has to call again
- Different departments need separate auths for the same patient episode
- After-hours pre-auth lines have longer wait times and less experienced staff

## Friction 3 — Patient Gap Billing and Collections

After the medical aid pays, the patient owes the gap. Collecting it is a second full billing cycle.

**The problems:**
- Patients do not understand the gap — "I have medical aid, why do I owe money?" — leads to disputes
- Patients ignore statements — billing person must follow up manually
- Phone calls are time-consuming and often unsuccessful
- SMS reminders are manual and inconsistent
- Accounts go 30, 60, 90 days overdue before anyone escalates
- Sending to debt collection damages the patient relationship and recovers a fraction of the amount
- Some patients dispute the gap amount months after the visit

**The math:** A practice with 40 patients a day at an average gap of R300 per patient has R12,000 a day in gap billing going out. Even 20% non-payment is R2,400 a day — R50,000+ a month in uncollected revenue.

## Friction 4 — Payment Reconciliation

Every week or two, the medical aid pays a lump sum. That payment covers hundreds of individual claims submitted over the previous period.

**What has to happen:**
- Download the remittance advice (EOB — Explanation of Benefits) from the medical aid portal
- Match each line on the remittance to the original claim in the billing software
- Identify short-payments and flag for query
- Identify claims not on the remittance at all (still pending or lost)
- Update each claim's status in the billing software
- Repeat for every medical aid (8–15 different schemes for a busy practice)

**The problem:** This is completely manual. Each medical aid has a different remittance format. Some send Excel files. Some send PDFs. Some require logging into a portal and downloading a report. A billing person can spend an entire day doing nothing but reconciliation.

---

## Friction 5 — Multiple Portal Logins

A practice dealing with 10 medical aids has 10 different portals to check:
- Different usernames and passwords
- Different interfaces
- Different report formats
- Different pre-auth processes
- Different rejection reason code systems

No single view of everything. Billing person switches between tabs all day. Things get missed.

---

## Friction 6 — Coding Errors at the Point of Capture

The doctor records the diagnosis and procedure on an encounter sheet or in the system. The billing person then codes it. Two failure points:

- Doctor records the diagnosis incorrectly or uses a description that maps to the wrong ICD-10 code
- Billing person picks the wrong code from a dropdown of thousands of options

One wrong code = rejection. The error is only discovered weeks later when the remittance comes back. By then the billing person has to reconstruct what happened during that consultation.

---

## Friction 7 — Tariff Knowledge

Each medical aid pays a different amount for the same procedure. The billing person needs to know:
- What each medical aid's tariff schedule says
- Whether the doctor's fee exceeds the tariff and by how much
- Whether a specific procedure is covered under the patient's specific plan
- Whether the patient has a savings account, day-to-day benefits, or hospital plan only

This knowledge is not centrally available. It lives in the billing person's head, in printed tariff booklets, and in years of experience. When that person leaves, the practice loses institutional knowledge that takes months to rebuild.

---

## Friction 8 — The Billing Person Leaving

This is the crisis every practice fears. One person holds all the knowledge:
- Which medical aids have pending queries
- Which patients have payment arrangements
- Which claims are on appeal
- Which patients dispute their gap
- Who to call at Discovery when something is wrong

When they leave: chaos. New billing person starts from scratch. Rejections pile up. Cash flow drops. Doctor panics.

There is no documentation, no handover process, no system that captures the institutional knowledge. It lives entirely in one person's head.

---

## Friction 9 — No Real-Time Visibility for the Doctor

The doctor sees patients all day. They have no idea:
- How much revenue was billed this week
- How much has been collected vs outstanding
- Which medical aids are paying slowly
- Which claims were rejected and why
- What their cash position looks like

They find out at month end when the practice manager or accountant reports. By then it is too late to fix problems from three weeks ago.

---

## Friction 10 — SARS and Practice Tax Administration

On top of all the medical aid billing, the practice has the same accounting problems as any other business:
- VAT returns (if registered)
- PAYE for staff
- Income tax for the doctor
- Monthly management accounts

Most practices use the same accountant for this as any other SME. Same document chasing, same deadline panic, same problems documented in the accounting sector analysis.
